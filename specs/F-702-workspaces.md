# F-702 · Workspaces

**Status:** APPROVED BEHAVIOR (2026-08-30; review amendment approved 2026-09-04) · **Implementation:** GATED by the reviewed machine contracts and F-701/F-703 work named below · see `docs/BASELINE.md`.

**Phase:** 2 · **Issue:** [#49](https://github.com/jzeng151/pop-engine/issues/49) · **Owner:** product owner · **Reviewer/approver:** product owner · **Approval date:** 2026-08-30

## Purpose and User Outcome

Authenticated organizers can group events and members inside an explicit workspace boundary so one organization's data cannot leak into another.

## Scope

**In scope**

- Create, name, view, and switch workspaces; manage membership invitations and acceptance.
- Derive workspace scope server-side for every authenticated aggregate.
- Require workspace ownership before authenticated user-owned product data is persisted.

**Non-goals**

- Role-specific permissions beyond the minimum owner needed to establish a workspace; F-703 owns authorization roles.
- Workspace billing, domains, SSO, nested organizations, or cross-workspace sharing.

## Inputs, Outputs, State, Validation, and Errors

- Inputs are an authenticated actor, workspace name, and invitation target; outputs are a workspace, membership, and server-selected active workspace context. An invitation target email is normalized by trimming surrounding whitespace, preserving the local part exactly, and lowercasing only the domain. Dots, plus tags, and provider-specific aliases are never folded.
- Invitation states and every transition between them, including who may perform each one and against which identity and version, are enumerated in F702-AC-08. Membership state is active → removed. Membership transitions, including who may perform each one and against whom, are enumerated in F702-AC-11; no ordinary transition may leave the workspace without an active owner. F-702 defines no inactive workspace state, workspace-state transition, or workspace deletion; switching is gated by active membership alone. Any later suspension, archival, or deletion behavior requires a separately approved amendment. F-702 has no role-change, account-deletion, or zero-owner recovery operation and never reassigns ownership automatically. Any future account deletion must preserve the same last-owner invariant and requires a separately approved F-701 amendment before it can ship.
- A client-supplied workspace identifier is never sufficient authorization. Every authenticated organizer aggregate query joins through active membership. The public attendee routes named in F702-AC-10 instead resolve the event server-side, derive tenancy through that event, accept no caller-supplied workspace, and return or write only their approved public projection or event-owned descendant.
- Missing or unresolved material data stays visibly unset, unknown, pending, or failed as appropriate; it never becomes a successful or complete result.
- Invalid input produces a field or action-specific error without partial mutation. Retriable external failures preserve the user's confirmed state and expose a safe retry.

## UI and Accessibility

- Workspace switcher and invitation screens identify the active workspace before any mutation and clearly distinguish expired, revoked, and unauthorized invitations.
- The complete workflow is keyboard operable, uses programmatic labels and visible focus, does not encode status by color alone, and announces asynchronous success or failure.
- Empty, loading, permission-denied, validation, provider-failure, and unavailable states have explicit copy and a safe next action.

## Acceptance Criteria

1. **F702-AC-01:** Workspace creation and active-owner membership insertion are atomic and bind a stable client-supplied request identity to the original result; replay returns that workspace and the current representation of the exact founding membership without creating another. That identity is committed under a uniqueness constraint scoped to the creating actor, not to a workspace, because the workspace it would be scoped to is what this operation creates. This is the one mutation F702-AC-10 does not reach, and F702-AC-11 M-00 is the membership transition it performs. That identity binds its operands under the rule `F702-AC-12` states once for every client-supplied identity this spec defines: it is committed together with the request fields that determine the recorded result, including every aggregate, version, and generation the request names, and a later request presenting the same identity with any different operand is refused as a conflict rather than being answered with the stored result. The identity is still the key and the operands are only a precondition on reusing it, so this is not the content uniqueness this criterion already forbids. That identity is resolved in the order `F702-AC-12` states once for every client-supplied identity this spec defines, applied here rather than restated: a request presenting an already committed identity is resolved from that record before the version, generation, state, and limit checks this criterion requires, and returns the workspace identified by that outcome together with the founding membership's current representation, because the commit the retry repeats is exactly what moved the state those checks read and a later F-703 role change must not be hidden. The acting actor's current authority is not among the checks the identity is resolved past: after the replay record identifies the created workspace, the server rechecks that the exact founding membership recorded in the outcome remains active and belongs to the creating actor before returning the workspace or current membership representation. Removal makes that replay unavailable permanently; a later replacement membership for the same actor and workspace does not reactivate it. A refusal discloses neither the workspace nor the stored outcome. A request whose identity resolves to no committed outcome is then held to every one of the first-attempt checks, and a request refused by one of them commits no identity, so re-presenting it later is a first attempt and not a replay.
2. **F702-AC-02:** Acceptance atomically compare-and-swaps the invitation from `pending` to `accepted` and returns the actor's one active membership of that workspace. The same transaction creates that membership only when none exists; its initial role is the role approved by F-703 and no new-member acceptance path may ship before that role contract is available. F-702 does not infer or define the role. When one active membership already exists, acceptance returns it unchanged and creates no row. Revocation competes on the same invitation row and version, so exactly one terminal transition wins. Expired, revoked, reused, mismatched, or pre-removal invitations create no membership and do not alter an existing one. "Mismatched" is defined by F702-AC-08 as the accepting actor's verified identity failing to equal the invitation's recorded target identity. "Pre-removal" means the invitation was issued before or at that actor's latest removal from the workspace under the durable ordering in T-13. Both checks occur inside the accepting transaction before the invitation transition and any optional membership creation. A first acceptance needs no prior membership because the verified target identity is its authority. T-11 replay additionally requires the membership returned by the original acceptance to remain active.
3. **F702-AC-03:** Every workspace-owned aggregate rejects cross-workspace reads, writes, identifier guessing, exports, uploads, and job execution.
4. **F702-AC-04:** Owner removal/leave serializes on the workspace (or uses an equivalent database invariant) so the last active owner cannot be removed under concurrent requests; the concurrent two-owner removal fixture leaves at least one owner. This criterion decides only that the invariant survives concurrency; who may perform each membership transition, and against whom, is F702-AC-11, and the authority check it names is made inside this same serialized transaction.
5. **F702-AC-05:** No authenticated user-owned product data or external beta is enabled before F-703 is also deployed and verified.
6. **F702-AC-06:** Issuing and revoking an invitation require the actor to hold an active owner membership of that exact workspace, checked server-side against stored membership rather than any client-supplied workspace or role claim. A non-owner member, a removed member, and a member of a different workspace are each rejected without disclosing whether the workspace or invitation exists, and the rejection creates, mutates, and expires nothing.

   This is the minimum owner authority the non-goals reserve to F-702 rather than a role model, which stays F-703's. Without it, AC-02 and AC-03 hold and the boundary still opens: AC-02 governs only transitions of an invitation that already exists, and AC-03 admits anyone who reached active membership. An ordinary member could therefore invite an outsider, the outsider would accept through AC-02 into a valid membership, and every cross-workspace check in AC-03 would pass for them, because by then they are legitimately inside. Membership is what AC-03 trusts, so the criterion that decides who may create membership cannot be deferred to F-703 while F-702 ships. Revocation is named alongside issuance for the same reason: leaving it open lets any member cancel an owner's pending invitation.

7. **F702-AC-07:** Issuing an invitation binds the request to a stable client-supplied request identity, committed with the invitation under a uniqueness constraint scoped to the workspace. A retry presenting the same identity returns the original invitation's current representation and original token and creates no second pending row. At or after the seven-day boundary, T-09 governs that read and the representation is `expired`, never `pending`. Deliberately inviting the same address again sends a new identity. This is request identity, never content uniqueness: two genuinely distinct invitations that read the same are both created, and a repeated identity is never rejected as a duplicate value. That identity binds its operands under the rule `F702-AC-12` states once for every client-supplied identity this spec defines: it is committed together with the request fields that determine the recorded result, including every aggregate, version, and generation the request names, and a later request presenting the same identity with any different operand is refused as a conflict rather than being answered with the stored result. The identity is still the key and the operands are only a precondition on reusing it, so this is not the content uniqueness this criterion already forbids. That identity is resolved in the order `F702-AC-12` states once for every client-supplied identity this spec defines, applied here rather than restated: a request presenting an already committed identity is resolved from that record before the version, generation, state, and limit checks this criterion requires, and returns the outcome that request originally recorded, because the commit the retry repeats is exactly what moved the state those checks read. Expiry is not a check the issuance commit changed, so T-02 applies T-09 before returning the current representation. The acting actor's current authority is not among the checks the identity is resolved past: the authority this feature requires for a first attempt is re-read server-side at the replay, and the stored outcome is returned only if it still admits the operation, so a replay presented after that authority is gone is refused and discloses no more than a first request would. A request whose identity resolves to no committed outcome is then held to every one of them, and a request refused by one of them commits no identity, so re-presenting it later is a first attempt and not a replay.

   AC-02 serializes transitions of one invitation and cannot see a second one. When issuance commits and its response is lost, the caller retries, a second pending row and a second token exist for the same intended invitation, and revoking or accepting either leaves the other still valid, so a revocation the owner was told succeeded does not close the invitation.

8. **F702-AC-08:** An invitation has exactly the states and transitions in the table below, and no operation outside that table creates, changes, or ends an invitation. The membership creation T-05 may perform is F702-AC-11 M-02; every later change to that membership or its authority is governed exclusively by F702-AC-11, including the F-703 role transition M-04 delegates. An invitation is in exactly one of `absent` (no record), `pending`, `accepted`, `revoked`, or `expired`; the last three are terminal, and no transition leaves a terminal state. Issuance records, immutably and at creation, the normalized intended target identity the invitation is for; that recorded target is never re-supplied by a later request and never changed after issuance. Normalization trims surrounding whitespace, preserves the local part exactly, lowercases only the domain, and performs no dot, plus-tag, or provider-specific alias folding. The same normalization runs at issuance and acceptance. Every state-changing transition names the invitation version it read and compare-and-swaps that version inside the transaction that performs it. A refused transition reads the named version and writes nothing.

   T-11 exists because T-05 and T-07 otherwise disagree about one request. When acceptance commits and its response is lost, the retry presents the same token and verified actor, but the invitation is now `accepted` rather than `pending`. T-07 would report the successful operation as reused or invalid whether T-05 created a membership or returned an existing one. Resolving the acceptance identity before the state comparison is the ordering F702-AC-12 states for every client-supplied identity in this spec. The state check cannot distinguish a retry from a genuine reuse because the commit being retried made the state terminal. The verified-identity comparison still runs. The replay also rechecks the membership returned by T-05 and refuses without returning the stored outcome if that membership is no longer active. When it remains active, the replay returns that membership's current representation rather than freezing its acceptance-time role; F-703 may change the same membership's role later. A presented identity whose actor does not equal the recorded target is refused on T-06's terms and returns nothing about the stored acceptance. An identity whose invitation or accepting actor differs from its committed operands is refused as a conflict under F702-AC-12. A retry without that committed identity is an ordinary T-07 refusal, and `accepted` remains terminal for every other actor.

   T-12 exists for the same reason on the other side of the same row, and until now revocation was the one transition in this table that changed an invitation and committed no identity of its own. T-01 issuance has the F702-AC-07 identity and T-05 acceptance has its own, so a revocation whose response is lost is the only invitation write whose retry has nothing to resolve against. That retry names the `pending` invitation and the version the owner read, both of which its own commit moved: the state is now `revoked` and terminal, so the transition finds no `pending` row to leave, and the version comparison finds the version already advanced. The owner is told the invitation was not pending, or that their revocation was stale, by the operation that in fact revoked it, at the moment they are trying to establish that a pending invitation is closed. The plausible next action, revoking again, is indistinguishable from a first revocation of an invitation someone else already revoked, so it does not settle the question either. Resolving the revocation identity before the state and version comparison is the ordering `F702-AC-12` states once for every client-supplied identity this spec defines, applied here rather than restated, and it grants nothing the original revocation was not granted: the owner-membership check T-08 requires is still made on that record's terms before anything is returned, so an actor who no longer holds an active owner membership is refused rather than answered, and an identity whose operands, the workspace and the invitation it named, do not match what was committed with it is refused as a conflict under `F702-AC-12` and revokes nothing. A retry carrying no committed identity against an invitation that is already `revoked` is an ordinary refusal on this table's terms, exactly as T-07 is for acceptance.

   T-13 prevents an invitation issued before removal from restoring the membership that removal ended without trying to infer an immutable actor from an email address. T-01 issuance and M-05 or M-06 removal receive a durable server-side order under the workspace serialization F702-AC-04 already requires. At acceptance, the verified Supabase subject identifies the actor. The transaction compares the invitation's issuance order with that actor's latest removal order in the workspace before changing the invitation or membership. An invitation issued before or at that removal is refused; one issued later is a distinct invitation and is evaluated normally. This comparison uses no caller-supplied clock, email-to-actor directory, or provider lookup. If the server cannot establish the ordering or the actor's removal history, acceptance fails closed and writes nothing.

   Each earlier round on this spec secured one step of this lifecycle and left the next, which is why the whole set is enumerated here rather than another rule being added beside them. F702-AC-06 fixed who may issue and revoke. F702-AC-07 fixed issuance replay. F702-AC-02 fixed the race between accepting and revoking one invitation that already exists. None of the three said who may accept: acceptance was reached by presenting the token, so the token was a bearer credential for entering the workspace, and a forwarded, leaked, or stolen one admitted whoever held it. Once inside, F702-AC-03 passes for that membership because it is a real membership, exactly as F702-AC-06 already described for an invitation issued by a non-owner. Delivery and expiry had no criterion at all: nothing said the delivery adapter may send only to the recorded target, so an implementation could accept a delivery address from the caller and bypass the binding T-05 checks, and nothing said an expired invitation is terminal at the moment of use, so an implementation could depend on a sweep the System Impact says workspace consistency must not depend on.

   The product owner approved the two invitation inputs on 2026-08-30. T-01 and T-05 use the same conservative normalization stated above, and T-09 uses seven days from issuance. F701-AC-05 now requires the actor projection to report the email value and explicit verified-email state. Its runtime and exact OpenAPI shape remain implementation prerequisites. Where the verified email or its verification state is absent, unverified, or unavailable, T-05 fails closed and creates no membership, because admitting an unverified identity would restore the bearer behavior this criterion removes.

9. **F702-AC-09:** Switching the active workspace names one target workspace. The server accepts the switch only when the actor holds an active membership of that exact workspace, checked server-side against stored membership rather than any client-supplied workspace or role claim. On success the server-selected active workspace context becomes that workspace, and every subsequent read and mutation derives its scope from the new context, so the switcher reaches each workspace the actor is actually a member of instead of remaining pinned to the first one. A target the actor is not an active member of, or whose membership was removed, is rejected without disclosing whether the workspace or the membership exists, and the previously active context is left unchanged.

   Switching selects among authority the actor already holds; it never creates, restores, or elevates any. It is not itself an authorization decision beyond the membership check above, and the authority for each later operation is still that actor's stored membership of the now-active workspace, re-read server-side per request rather than carried forward from the previously active workspace or frozen into the session at switch time. Being an owner of one workspace therefore confers nothing in another, a membership removed after a switch takes effect on the next request, and F702-AC-03 still rejects every request naming an aggregate outside the active workspace, so the active context narrows what a request may reach and never widens it. This is stated because the same identity-scoping defect has already appeared once on this stack: an operation that resolved authority from something other than the actor's own stored membership of the exact target. A switch that returned a context the server then trusted in place of a per-request membership check would be that defect again, with the client choosing the scope.

10. **F702-AC-10:** This criterion governs operations performed _inside_ an existing workspace: every mutation of a workspace-owned aggregate, and every externally initiated callback, whose authority is the actor's stored membership of that workspace. Each such operation names the workspace it acts in. That named workspace is validated against the actor's authority at the moment of the write, re-read server-side from stored membership rather than from the session, from a client-supplied role claim, or from the context that was active when the operation began. Switching the active workspace never retroactively changes what an in-flight operation acts on: an operation that named workspace A commits in workspace A or is rejected, and never in workspace B.

    Three operation classes fall outside that membership-authorized scope because stored workspace membership is not their source of authority. Each is governed by the approved criterion named beside it. **Workspace creation**, F702-AC-01, brings the workspace and its first membership into existence, so there is no prior workspace for the request to name and no stored membership to check. F-701 authentication alone authorizes the first attempt, and its replay identity is scoped to the creating actor because no workspace exists yet. Once a replay identifies the created workspace, AC-01 requires the exact founding membership recorded in the outcome to remain active before returning it. **Invitation acceptance**, F702-AC-08 T-05, may target an actor who has no membership or who already has one. The invitation supplies the workspace server-side, never the client. Authority for a first acceptance comes from comparing the actor's F-701 verified identity with the immutable recorded target, not from any membership the actor may already hold. T-13 also refuses an invitation issued before or at that actor's latest removal from the workspace. The transaction moves an admitted invitation from `pending` to `accepted` and returns the actor's one active membership; it creates that membership only when none exists and F-703's approved initial-role contract is available, otherwise it returns the existing membership unchanged. T-11 replays the stored acceptance only while that returned membership remains active. **Public event page, RSVP, and check-in**, approved F301-AC-06, F302-AC-01/F302-AC-06, and F401-AC-02/F401-AC-07, remain membership-free only while their exact route-specific Cloudflare Access rehearsal bypass is open. The public page read and API operations resolve the event named by the route, require the event and the route's approved publication/date conditions, derive workspace scope through that event, and accept no caller-supplied workspace. The page read returns only F-301's public projection; the public writes can create or update only the RSVP or check-in row owned by that event. Organizer guest, contact, intake, and workspace data stay behind membership authorization. Outside the recorded window, Cloudflare stops the anonymous request before the web or API origin.

    These exceptions do not permit a caller to choose tenancy. Creation writes only the new workspace and founding owner membership. Acceptance writes the invitation transition and, only when needed, the membership. The public page reads only the server-resolved event projection, and public RSVP and check-in write only beneath that event. Every authenticated workspace aggregate write remains inside this criterion.

    The scope sentence comes first because the earlier wording, "every mutation," reached two bootstrap operations it cannot govern. Workspace creation could satisfy the tenancy rule only by inventing a client-selected workspace identifier and an authorization path this spec does not define. Invitation acceptance could satisfy it only by treating a membership as the authority, even though the invitation's verified target match is the authority and the membership may not exist until that transaction commits. A rule that forbids a legal operation is as defective as one that permits an unsafe operation, so the scope is corrected here rather than handled through an exception list.

    This is the tenancy boundary rule for the whole feature set, stated once here and referenced rather than restated wherever a workspace-scoped operation or an external callback needs it. Four obligations follow from it, and an implementation satisfies the criterion only by meeting all four.

    First, naming. Every request that writes carries the workspace it was composed against, and every externally initiated callback carries a server-issued reference that resolves to one workspace. Naming is not authorization: the named workspace only narrows what the request may reach, and the actor's stored membership of that exact workspace is what admits it, exactly as F702-AC-09 already requires of a switch. A request whose named workspace differs from the server-selected active context is rejected and returned for the actor to recompose, and is never silently redirected into the currently active workspace. The rejection discloses nothing about whether the named workspace or its aggregates exist, per F702-AC-03.

    F101-AC-11 event creation follows that order before replay resolution. The server validates the named workspace and current active membership inside the transaction, then looks up the `Idempotency-Key` only within that workspace. The stored key operands include the workspace and exact request body. A key committed in another workspace is neither a replay nor a conflict in the current one, returns no foreign event or stored-operation detail, and does not prevent an independent create using the same UUID. Migration 015's global constraint is not edited; the reviewed F-702 forward migration must replace it only after every retained event has a workspace and `events.workspace_id` has been validated and made non-null.

    Second, validation at the moment of the write. The membership check is made inside the transaction that commits, against stored membership read server-side at that instant, not at session start, not at switch time, and not at the moment the client began composing. A membership removed while a request was in flight therefore causes that request to fail rather than commit on authority the actor no longer holds.

    Third, callbacks. An externally initiated callback is not composed by the actor at all, so it cannot carry the actor's current context. Its workspace binding is instead established when the flow starts: the server issues and stores an unguessable single-use state bound to the initiating actor, the workspace, the provider, and the exact approved redirect target, and the callback is admitted only when it presents that state, which is consumed exactly once. `F212-AC-06` states this shape in full and `F308-AC-11` and `F408-AC-10` require it by reference; a callback with no such binding acts in whatever workspace the browser session happens to be in, which is the attacker-chosen workspace whenever the attacker started the flow.

    Fourth, co-tenancy of everything the request names. An operation that names more than one record requires every record it names to resolve to the same workspace, compared server-side inside the transaction that commits the operation and, for a read, at the operation that returns a value derived from any of them. The three obligations above are all about the actor: they ask where the actor stands and whether that authority is current. This one is about the objects, and it is the direction the actor check cannot see, because an actor holding an active membership of workspace A and an active membership of workspace B does nothing they are not entitled to do in either place separately while pairing a record from one with a record from the other. Every check written on the actor therefore passes, every named record genuinely exists, and the result of the operation carries one workspace's data into the other's.

    The obligation covers every record the request supplies independently of the aggregate the authority criterion binds, whatever the spec calls it: another aggregate, a pinned version or generation, a source document, a selected recipient or contact snapshot, a subject membership, a run or proposal set, and a target the operation is about to create. It is not satisfied by a check that the named record exists, by a compare-and-swap proving the named version is current, or by a request identity whose operands include it, because a record in another workspace exists, its version is current there, and the operands match what was committed. A mismatch rejects the whole operation before any durable write, mutates nothing, appends no history, projection, generation, job, or audit entry, discloses no field of any named record, and returns a response that does not distinguish a record that does not exist from one that resolves to a different workspace, per F702-AC-03.

    The records the request supplies are not all the records the comparison covers. An operation also reaches records it never names, through the records it does: a channel endpoint through the contact it belongs to, a provider object through the mapping that targets it, a prior artifact through the lineage it sits in, an approval or a run through the artifact it was granted against. Those are operands of the operation as much as the named ones, because they are what the write finally lands on, and no reading of a criterion's own prose can enumerate them, since the criterion does not mention them. Each of them is compared to its own immediate parent, and through that parent to the scope above, so the chain is closed rather than each link being tied separately to a common ancestor: binding two records to one event does not make one of them a record of the other. The comparison is made wherever the operation acts, which for a deferred operation is at every point it acts: when a set is assembled or a mapping selected, again inside the transaction that commits, and again immediately before each provider call, job execution, or delivery, because the relationship can be true at one of those and false at the next.

    A scope stored beside a record is not the comparison either, and this is the direction that is easiest to miss. Where a record carries a workspace, jurisdiction, or event as a column rather than reaching one through a parent, that column is derived from the record it scopes inside the transaction that writes it, not supplied alongside it and validated on its own. A row written with an aggregate from one scope and a scope value from another satisfies every requirement that both fields be present and name something real, and it is then filtered on the value that is wrong: it appears where it does not belong, or a defensive read-time comparison hides it from both places and leaves the owning scope's history incomplete with no criterion permitting a repair.

    Where a spec's own aggregate is narrower than a workspace, the comparison is made at that narrower scope as well, not instead. The event is that scope throughout this feature set: one workspace holds many events, so two records in one workspace satisfy the workspace comparison while belonging to different events, and a spec whose aggregate is an event compares the event and the workspace both. `F104-AC-04`, `F109-AC-07`, `F406-AC-09`, and `F407-AC-08` state the pair that way, and `F105-AC-08`, `F214-AC-09`, `F604-AC-09`, `F502-AC-08`, and `F503-AC-07` state the workspace half for the records they name.

    The scope of each comparison is set by the slot that consumes the record, not by the widest scope the feature legitimately spans. Reading "the narrowest aggregate this spec owns" as the feature's span is what leaves a multi-event, multi-contact, or multi-milestone feature fenced at the workspace and nothing narrower, and the reasoning looks sound at each site because a fence at the span's own aggregate really would break the feature: an event fence makes a cross-event analytics refresh, a cross-event comparison, a workspace-wide contact export, and a workspace-wide calendar connection uncomputable for exactly the records they exist to show. The conclusion drawn from that is the error. A span says which records may be gathered and never says which one a gathered record may stand in for. Every value a gathered record supplies still lands in one slot, one comparison column, one exported row, one provider entry, one replaced current pointer, and the comparison is against the record that owns that slot: the event whose column consumes a snapshot, the contact whose row consumes a consent version, the milestone whose sync consumes a mapping. That comparison is available to a spanning feature exactly as it is to a single-aggregate one, because the slot exists either way.

    A criterion is read leg by leg for this, not as a whole. Where one leg resolves a record through its owner and another lets the caller name the record directly, only the first leg carries the comparison, and it carries it incidentally rather than by being required to. A per-event current pointer, a per-contact current version, a per-milestone existing mapping each land in the right slot because the lookup started from the owner; the explicit-selection, historical-version, or caller-supplied alternative beside them starts from the record instead and must state the comparison the default leg never had to. The correctly bound default is what makes the unbound alternative invisible to a reader checking whether the criterion binds its records, so the leg that lets a caller name a record is the one to read.

    This obligation is stated here because it was found one instance at a time and repeated in nine proposed specs while F-702 was not an approved input. Approval on 2026-08-30 makes F702-AC-10 the shared workspace-tenancy rule those specs may cite. Replacing their local copies is derived-document cleanup, not part of this approval package. The separate identity rule in F702-AC-12 still cannot cite F-411 because F-411 depends on F-702, and the shared replay ADR remains deferred.

    Without this criterion, F702-AC-03 and F702-AC-09 both hold and the boundary still opens. F702-AC-03 rejects cross-workspace access by comparing an existing aggregate's workspace to the active one, so it has nothing to compare for a create, which has no aggregate yet, and the actor is legitimately a member of both workspaces so no membership check fails. F702-AC-09 then makes the active context mutable without giving any subsequent request a way to say which context it was composed against. A member with workspace A open in one tab and workspace B in another therefore switches to B and the next create from the A tab, composed entirely against A and showing A's name in the switcher F702-AC-09 requires, writes into B. Nothing rejects it, nothing records that it was misdirected, and one organizer's data is now in another organizer's workspace. The same reasoning covers an external callback, where the gap is wider still because the party that chooses when the callback arrives is outside the boundary.

    Operations that are not workspace-scoped do not satisfy this criterion by naming a workspace. The platform rules-admin surfaces F-711 and F-712 define name their own platform scope, which `F703-AC-04` keeps separate from and ungrantable by a workspace owner, and `F711-AC-06` already states why a workspace-scoped identity would be wrong for them. Such an operation is in scope for this criterion only in that it must name a scope and validate authority for that scope at the moment of the write; the scope it names is the platform one.

    Each earlier round on this spec closed one hole in this authority model and left the next, which is why the whole set is enumerated here rather than another rule being added beside them. F702-AC-01 fixed ownership at creation and its replay. F702-AC-06 fixed who may issue and revoke. F702-AC-07 fixed issuance replay. F702-AC-08 T-05 fixed who may accept. F702-AC-04 fixed only that the last-owner invariant survives concurrency, and said nothing about who may perform a removal, while T-10 delegated the whole removal and leave transition back to it. Removal is the most damaging of these, because an ordinary member who can remove others can remove every owner up to the last one and then hold a workspace the owners can no longer administer, and because F702-AC-03 admits whoever holds an active membership, so nothing downstream rejects the survivor.

    The table shows a fifth hole nobody has reported, and M-04 states it. The last-owner invariant is written over removal and leave, so it is evaded by a transition that ends ownership without ending membership: demote the last active owner to an ordinary member and the workspace has no owner, no membership was removed, F702-AC-04 never applies, and every criterion above still holds. Nobody can then issue an invitation under F702-AC-06, revoke one under T-08, or remove anyone under M-05, and no criterion in this spec restores ownership. F-702 cannot fix this by defining a role model, because its non-goals reserve that to F-703; what it can do, and what M-04 does, is bind the invariant to any transition that ends an actor's owner authority rather than to the removal verb, so F-703's role change inherits it instead of routing around it.

    The product owner approved the role and recovery boundary on 2026-08-30. F-702 defines no role-change operation, so M-04 binds the last-owner invariant to later F-703 transitions without authorizing one here. Every ordinary removal, leave, and later demotion path must refuse a change that would remove the last active owner. Any future account-deletion path must preserve that same invariant, but this spec neither activates nor defines one. F-702 provides no zero-owner recovery workflow and never assigns ownership automatically; a later recovery path requires its own approval rather than an inferred fallback.

Any later acceptance by that Supabase subject compares the invitation's issuance order with the latest removal order for the actor and workspace. An older or equal invitation cannot restore membership. A later invitation is a new T-01 with its own issuance order, token, version, and request identity.

12. **F702-AC-12:** Every client-supplied request identity this spec defines is committed together with the operands that determine the result recorded under it, and a reuse that does not match those operands is a conflict and not a replay. The operands are the request fields the recorded result depends on, at minimum the workspace or the creating actor the identity is scoped to, the aggregate the request names, and every version, generation, and state the request names. A request presenting a committed identity is a replay only when every one of those operands equals what was stored with it. It receives the durable outcome identified by that record, subject to the current-authority, lifecycle, and current-representation checks that its transition keeps live. AC-01 therefore returns the current representation of the exact founding membership. T-02 returns the original invitation's current representation after T-09 applies expiry; it never reports an expired invitation as pending. T-11 likewise returns the same membership's current representation rather than its acceptance-time role. When any operand differs, the request is refused as a conflict: it receives no stored outcome, commits nothing, creates or changes no workspace, invitation, or membership, and is neither treated as a replay nor allowed to proceed as a fresh operation under that identity. The refusal names the conflict without disclosing the stored operation's workspace, invitation, target, subject, or outcome, per F702-AC-03's non-disclosure rule, and the caller recovers by sending a new identity for the operation it actually intends. This is not content uniqueness: the identity stays the key the client chooses and the operands are only a precondition on reusing it, so two genuinely distinct operations that read the same are both committed under two identities, while one identity can only ever mean one operation. Without it, an identity is a replay key the client controls and the server honors for operands it never computed the outcome against, and returning a stored outcome for different operands is a way to make one operation masquerade as another rather than a form of idempotency.

    A committed identity is resolved before any version, generation, state, or limit check whose answer the committed operation itself changed. The identity is looked up first, against the record the committing transaction wrote; only a request whose identity resolves to no committed outcome is then evaluated against the checks that guard a first attempt, and only a request passing those is admitted and commits its outcome and identity together. A state check made first cannot tell a retry from a fresh request, because the commit the retry is repeating is exactly what moved the state that check reads: the version it names has been advanced by that commit, the pending state it requires has become terminal, or the membership that commit itself removed is gone. Every one of those rejects work that in fact succeeded and reports a completed operation to its caller as stale, conflicting, or invalid.

    The acting actor's current authority is not one of the checks the identity is resolved past. A replay rechecks the authority named by its transition after resolving the committed identity and before returning the stored outcome. T-02 and T-12 therefore recheck active owner membership. M-07 rechecks the membership required by M-05 or M-06, except that a committed M-06 self-leave satisfies its own membership read because that operation removed the membership. Workspace-creation replay requires the exact founding membership recorded by F702-AC-01 to remain active after the identity identifies the workspace. T-11 is different because T-05's verified target identity, not membership, authorizes the first acceptance. T-11 repeats that identity check, requires the membership returned by T-05 to remain active, and returns its current representation; a removed actor receives no stored acceptance outcome. These rules let bootstrap operations create their first membership without allowing a later replacement membership to expose an earlier stored outcome.

    Resolving the identity first weakens none of the state checks. A resolved identity returns the durable result that was already computed under the checks its commit changed, only after the current-authority and live lifecycle checks above run, and grants nothing the actor may not have at replay. An unresolved identity is admitted only by passing every first-attempt check, and a resolved identity whose operands do not match is refused as a conflict under the paragraph above rather than admitted to either path. A request refused by one of those checks commits no identity, so re-presenting it later is a first attempt and not a replay. Every replay guarantee in this spec covers exactly the identities that reached a committed outcome. F702-AC-01 applies it to workspace creation, F702-AC-07 to invitation issuance, F702-AC-08 T-11 to an acceptance whose replay a not-pending check would reject and T-12 to a revocation whose replay the same check would reject, and F702-AC-11 M-07 to a self-leave whose replay a membership precheck would deny.

    This rule is stated here in full rather than by reference to `F411-AC-08`, which states the same rule once for the specs that depend on F-411, and `F703-AC-03` states it locally for the same reason F-703 gives. The Dependencies and Baseline section explains why this spec cannot take that reference: F-411 declares F-702 as a dependency, so a dependency in the other direction would make the two specs blockers of each other and neither could be approved first. The rule is not about workspaces any more than it is about door credentials, so stating it in three places is a cost of it having no approved home rather than a design. The product owner deliberately deferred promotion to a shared invariant on 2026-08-30. A later promotion remains a durable architecture decision that needs an approved ADR under `docs/DOCUMENTATION-GOVERNANCE.md` §6.

13. **F702-AC-13:** An actor holds at most one active membership of a workspace at any time. The invariant is enforced by a database uniqueness constraint over the actor and the workspace, restricted to active memberships so that a membership already `removed` under F702-AC-11 M-05 or M-06 does not block a later re-invitation, and it is checked inside the same serialized transaction F702-AC-04 requires. T-01 may issue an invitation when the target already holds an active membership. Acceptance under F702-AC-08 T-05 and F702-AC-11 M-02 then marks the invitation `accepted`, returns the existing active membership unchanged, changes no role, and creates no membership row.

    This is a cardinality rule and not a tenancy one, and it is stated separately because the workspace boundary rules above are all satisfied while it is violated. T-01 deliberately permits an owner to issue another invitation to the same recorded target identity with a new request identity, since F702-AC-07's identity is a retry key and not content uniqueness, and re-inviting someone is a legitimate action when the first invitation was lost, expired, or revoked. Nothing in T-05 then compares the accepting actor against the memberships that already exist: the verified identity matches the recorded target, the invitation is `pending`, the acceptance identity is fresh, and M-02's "must remain true afterwards" column requires exactly one active membership _for that invitation_, which two rows created by two invitations each satisfy on their own.

    The harm is that every downstream rule on this branch reads "an active membership" and finds one. F702-AC-03 admits the holder of any active membership, and F702-AC-10's actor check re-reads stored membership and finds a live row. So removal under M-05, leave under M-06, and role change under M-04 each act on one row and leave the other untouched and active: an owner who removes a member sees the removal commit and the audit entry written, and the actor keeps the access the owner believes was withdrawn, through the row the removal did not name. M-07's replay makes the same mistake safely idempotent rather than visible. The count invariants are also read per row, so a demotion under M-04 can be reported as ending an actor's ownership while a second active owner membership for the same actor still stands.

    Two consequences are stated rather than left implied. First, the F702-AC-04 last-active-owner invariant counts distinct actors holding active owner memberships and not active owner rows, so two rows for one actor are one owner for the purpose of the check that a workspace is never left without one; with this criterion in force the two cannot coexist, and the counting rule is stated because an implementation that enforces uniqueness by cleanup rather than by constraint would otherwise let a transient duplicate satisfy it. Second, the constraint is what enforces this and not an application-level read-then-write, for the reason F208-AC-01 gives for its own slot: two concurrent acceptances of two invitations to the same target each read no existing membership and each insert one.

    The product owner chose the existing-membership resolution on 2026-08-30. Issuance remains allowed. Acceptance succeeds, makes the invitation terminal, returns the held membership, and leaves its role unchanged. On 2026-09-04 the product owner kept the initial role for a newly created membership in F-703 rather than inventing one here. Until F-703 approves and ships that value, T-05 can return an existing membership but cannot create a new one. This keeps invitation replay deterministic without turning F-702 into a second role authority.

14. **F702-AC-14:** Tenancy activates through a staged rollout while the joint production gate stays closed. First, a reviewed forward migration creates the workspace and membership storage and adds nullable `events.workspace_id`, but creates no workspace or membership. Second, the product owner authenticates and uses F702-AC-01 and M-00 to create the explicitly identified synthetic workspace and its active owner membership. Third, the reviewed backfill names that existing workspace, verifies its active owner, verifies that every event it will attach is synthetic capstone data, and assigns those existing events to it. The migration then verifies that no retained event has a null workspace, validates the column, and makes `events.workspace_id` non-null before it replaces migration 015's global replay-key constraint. A failed validation aborts without dropping the global constraint. Under the approved AD-12 amendment, `events` is the sole current user-owned aggregate root. Existing event-owned descendants, including plans, checklist items, documents, alerts, RSVPs, and check-ins, derive scope through their required event relationship and receive no duplicate workspace column; public-page state already lives on the event. A future independent user-owned aggregate root carries its own `workspace_id`. Global rules, provider data, and configuration are not backfilled into a workspace. Any missing workspace, inactive owner, non-synthetic event, or unresolved precondition aborts the backfill without attaching or deleting data. Exact table, column, constraint, and migration shapes remain subject to later product-owner approval.
